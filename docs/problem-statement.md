## THE PROBLEM

During floods, heatwaves and other disasters, warnings may not reach everyone. Elderly residents, people living alone, low-income neighbourhoods, people with disabilities and those without smartphones or reliable signal are the least likely to receive alerts and the most likely to be in danger. At the same time, rescue teams, vehicles, shelters and medical supplies are limited, so every decision about who to help first carries a cost.

## THE CHALLENGE

## CORE CHALLENGE

How can technology identify who needs help most and coordinate available resources to reach them before the situation becomes critical?

## KEY QUESTIONS TO THINK ABOUT

| Who is most at risk? | Vulnerability depends on location, age, health, mobility, housing and access to information. Teams must decide what signals reveal risk and how to find people when warnings do not reach everyone. |
| --- | --- |
| Who gets help first? | Rescue teams, vehicles, shelters and medical supplies are finite. The system must match scarce resources to the greatest need, and justify its priority order in a way responders can trust. |
| How early can you act? | Help is most effective before a situation becomes critical. Think about early signals, forecasts, crowd reports and pre-positioning of resources rather than reacting after the fact. |

## WHO IS AFFECTED

- Residents of flood-prone and low-lying areas with no early warning

- Elderly, disabled, chronically ill or isolated people who cannot self-evacuate

- Rescue coordinators and volunteers with limited vehicles and supplies

- Shelter and relief managers who must balance capacity against demand

## WHERE IT GETS HARD

- Incomplete information: reports will be late, duplicated, inaccurate or missing entirely from some areas.

- Competing priorities: a medical emergency, a stranded family and a flooded road all demand the same team.

- Connectivity failure: networks and power often fail exactly when the system is needed.
